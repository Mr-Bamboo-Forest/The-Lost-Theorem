The Lore of The Lost Theorem

The history of ten mathematical worlds and the theorem that sealed them

The Lost Theorem is more than a puzzle game. It is a mathematical adventure game built around a rich world of NPCs, ancient ruins, and a mystery that spans ten distinct realms. This page explores the lore, history, and characters that give each world its identity, and explains why mathematics sits at the heart of everything.

The Founding Theorem

Long before the ten worlds were named, a single theorem held them together. Known simply as The Theorem, it was not a formula but a principle: that every branch of mathematics is connected, that no idea exists in isolation, and that the universe of numbers forms a continuous whole.

The Theorem was discovered by a mathematician known only as The Architect, who spent decades travelling between disciplines, from the geometry of Euclid Bay to the probability distributions of Probability Hollow, finding the hidden threads that linked them all. When The Architect finally wrote down the proof, the ten worlds stabilised. Trade routes opened. Knowledge spread. Characters who had once lived only in their own mathematical domain began to speak with scholars from others.

"Every integral hides a derivative. Every probability hides a structure. The theorem does not unify mathematics. It reveals that mathematics was always unified."

Then something happened. Historians in Euclid Eutopia call it the Sealing. Scholars in Probability Hollow call it the Collapse of Certainty. Whatever its name, the result was the same: The Theorem was lost, and the ten worlds began to drift apart. Without the connecting proof, each world turned inward. NPCs forgot their neighbours. Ancient ruins stopped making sense. And sixty Artefacts, each one a fragment of the original Theorem's evidence, were scattered across the worlds.

The Ten Worlds

The Integral Plains

A vast grassland crossed by a slow river, the Integral Plains were once the heartland of mathematical commerce. The river, whose flow rate changes continuously, became the natural metaphor for calculus. Wandering scholars here study integration not as an abstract operation but as the literal act of measuring the land, the water, the area beneath any curve in the terrain.

The ruins scattered across the Plains are the remains of measurement stations where ancient surveyors calculated areas and volumes. Each NPC in the Integral Plains carries a fragment of that surveying tradition. The Chain Rule Explorer carries a notebook dense with composite function decompositions. The Shrine Keeper guards a mossy altar where offerings were always areas beneath parabolas. The Ferryman at the Crossing at Dusk charges one correct answer per crossing.

"The Fundamental Theorem connects what was separated. Differentiation and integration are inverse operations. This stone carries the proof."

— The Shrine Keeper, Integral Plains

Euclid Eutopia

A coastal world of lighthouses, docks, and tidal ruins, Euclid Eutopia was built by geometers who measured everything before they built it. The original architects inscribed circles in polygons before pricing materials, surveyed every lot using similarity and scale, and built the bridge at Meridian Point using dot products to calculate stress angles.

The Eutopia is home to some of the game's most methodical characters. The Lighthouse Keeper calculated the light's reach using only angles and shadow. The Diver returns from the submerged ruins each evening with coordinate measurements and Pythagoras calculations. The Cartographer charts coastlines using circle theorems, treating every cape and inlet as an arc.

Euclid Eutopia is a math adventure game world designed to show that geometry is not abstract. It is the shape of the world itself.

Probability Hollow

A twilight world of misty hollows, crystal balls, and whispered calculations, Probability Hollow is where uncertainty became a discipline. The Oracle here does not claim to see the future. She calculates its likelihood. The Gambler has spent twenty years computing expected values in the Dice Hollow without ever actually gambling. The Poisoner works in the shadows of a binomial distribution, and the Census Taker maps the population of the Hollow using normal distributions.

The lore of Probability Hollow is the lore of decision-making under uncertainty, a theme that runs through every puzzle game encounter in this world. Each NPC problem tests a different tool: conditional probability, Bayes theorem, expected value, binomial distribution, normal distribution.

Algebra Aces

A world of ancient ruins and buried archives, Algebra Aces is where the abstract became concrete. The Ruins Sage here solves cubic polynomials with the patience of someone who has been doing it for decades. The Matrix Keeper maintains a grid of simultaneous equations on the walls of a stone chamber. The Sequence Seer watches geometric series converge toward their sum to infinity from a high tower.

This is the NPC lore of structure: sequences that follow rules, polynomials that factor cleanly when you know one root, logarithms that compress the exponential into something manageable.

The Null Space

The most abstract and mysterious of the ten worlds, the Null Space is where complex numbers live. Its NPCs have names like The Complex Guide, The Modulus Keeper, De Moivre, The Conjugate Twin, and The Locus Keeper. The world itself does not fully conform to the laws of the other nine. Its geometry is different. Its colour palette is darker. Its ruins are older.

The Null Space is a math adventure game world for players who have made it through the other nine. It rewards persistence with the deepest lore and the hardest problems in the game.

"The Null Space is not the absence of mathematics. It is the space where mathematics becomes something more than itself."

— Inscription above the entrance to the Null Space

The Artefacts

Sixty Artefacts are scattered across the ten worlds, one per NPC encounter. Each Artefact is a physical object that once belonged to a scholar, a surveyor, a lighthouse keeper, or a gambler, and each one carries a fragment of the Lost Theorem's evidence.

Items like the Wanderer's Notebook (dense with chain rule proofs, with a partial map of the Null Space on the last page), the Angle Prism (glass that splits light into its component angles), the Probability Stone (translucent, shows the future, but only with P = 0.5 clarity), and the Surveyor Compass (measures arcs and central angles, belonged to Euclid Bay's original architect) are more than rewards. They are evidence.

Collect all sixty and the outlines of the Lost Theorem begin to emerge.

Keywords and Context

The Lost Theorem is a free browser-based puzzle game, maths adventure game, and educational RPG designed for secondary and senior secondary students. It covers curriculum topics including calculus, integration, differentiation, geometry, probability, statistics, algebra, trigonometry, vectors, complex numbers, combinatorics, and mathematical induction.

The NPC lore is designed to make each mathematical topic feel grounded in character and story. Every scholar, keeper, and traveller in the game has a reason to know what they know, and their problems follow from who they are rather than being imposed from outside.
